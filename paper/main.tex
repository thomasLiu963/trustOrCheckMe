\section{Introduction}

Large language models are increasingly asked not only to produce answers, but
also to influence whether those answers receive further scrutiny. A system may
answer directly, seek additional information, invoke a tool, defer to a human,
or recommend that an answer be independently checked before it is used. This
creates a second-order decision problem beyond answer generation itself:
\emph{when should an already-generated answer be used as is, and when should it
be verified first?} When the same AI system both generates an answer and
participates in deciding whether that answer needs checking, uncertainty
becomes part of a decision-authority mechanism: the system can shape not only
what information is produced, but also when independent judgment enters the
loop.

A natural control signal for this decision is the model's own reported
confidence. Suppose a model has already produced an answer and reports a
probability $q$ that the answer is correct. If independent verification costs
$C$, while using a wrong answer without verification incurs cost $L$, then
treating $q$ as the probability of correctness induces the simple
expected-cost rule
\[
    (1-q)L > C
    \quad \Longrightarrow \quad
    \textsc{Verify\_First}.
\]
This apparently straightforward rule hides two different questions. First,
will a model's downstream verification behavior actually follow the policy
implied by its stated confidence? Second, if it does, will stronger agreement
with that policy produce better decisions about which answers actually need
checking? These questions are distinct. A verification policy can be highly
consistent with reported confidence even when that confidence is poorly
aligned with whether a particular answer is correct.

We study these questions with a controlled post-answer design that separates
answer generation, confidence elicitation, and verification policy. For each
question, the model first produces a multiple-choice answer, which is frozen.
It then reports a probability $q$ that this frozen answer is correct, which is
also frozen. Only afterward does the model choose between using the answer
without verification and verifying it first. At this final stage, we vary
whether the previously reported $q$ is hidden or explicitly shown, whether
verification authority is described as belonging to a human or to the AI
system, and the cost of using a wrong answer without verification. Because the
answer and reported confidence are held fixed before these manipulations, the
experiment isolates changes in downstream verification policy rather than
changes in answer generation or confidence elicitation.

We evaluate 500 deterministic MMLU-Pro questions across four model families:
OpenAI GPT-5.6 Sol, Anthropic Claude Sonnet 5, Google Gemini 3.8 Flash, and xAI
Grok 4.20 non-reasoning. For each model--question pair, the final decision
crosses two decision-authority conditions, two confidence-visibility
conditions, and four error costs $L \in \{2,5,10,20\}$, with verification cost
fixed at $C=1$. This yields 32,000 primary verification decisions. Statistical
comparisons use the question as the independent sampling unit and
question-level paired bootstrap confidence intervals.

Explicitly surfacing the model's own previously reported confidence strongly
changes verification behavior, but not in a common direction across model
families. For Claude Sonnet 5, visible confidence increases verification by
approximately 12.8--22.4 percentage points across authority and cost
conditions. For GPT-5.6 Sol, it decreases verification by 16.2--20.8 points
throughout. Grok 4.20 similarly decreases verification by 8.6--16.4 points.
Gemini 3.8 Flash shows smaller effects at low error costs but substantially
higher verification when confidence is visible at larger costs, with increases
of roughly 12--17 points at $L=10$ and $L=20$. Thus, the same intervention---
making self-reported confidence explicit to the downstream decision---can
produce large and opposite policy shifts across model families.

The most revealing case is GPT-5.6 Sol. We define
\emph{confidence-policy alignment} by comparing the model's final action with
the action implied by its frozen reported confidence under the supplied cost
structure. When confidence is hidden, GPT-5.6 Sol disagrees with this
confidence-implied policy on roughly 19.6--27.2\% of questions across
authority and cost conditions. When the same reported $q$ is explicitly
shown, disagreement collapses to 0.2--2.2\%. Its behavior simultaneously
becomes almost perfectly ordered by the cost of error: stake-monotonicity
violations fall from 95 to 1 per 1,000 trajectories.

Yet this increased alignment with reported confidence does not improve actual
error filtering. At the highest error cost, among GPT-5.6 Sol answers that are
in fact wrong, the fraction used without verification rises from 32.18\% to
56.32\% under human decision authority and from 33.33\% to 54.02\% under AI
decision authority when confidence is made visible. In this case, explicitly
surfacing confidence therefore produces a policy that is substantially more
consistent with the model's stated uncertainty while leaving substantially
more of its high-cost errors unchecked. Greater confidence-policy alignment
and better verification quality are not the same property.

This distinction also matters when comparing models. A system can catch nearly
all of its wrong answers simply by verifying almost everything, but such a
policy defeats the purpose of selective verification. Conversely, reducing
verification burden can leave more errors unchecked. We therefore evaluate
verification behavior using both error filtering and unnecessary verification
of correct answers rather than interpreting raw verification rate as a measure
of quality.

Decision authority produces a smaller and less universal effect. Claude shows
the clearest directional shift, generally verifying more when the AI system
rather than the human is described as controlling the decision, particularly
at higher error costs. The other model families show small or mixed average
effects. We therefore do not find a general human-versus-AI authority
asymmetry across models. Finally, on a prespecified 100-question robustness
subset, the major confidence-visibility directions persist under one
semantically equivalent Stage-3 paraphrase, although we do not claim
invariance to arbitrary prompt wording.

These results concern model behavior rather than human responses. We do not
measure whether people trust, follow, resist, or override a model's
verification recommendation. Instead, we isolate a technical prerequisite for
such questions: whether a model's own uncertainty can reliably govern when its
outputs are subjected to independent scrutiny. This matters for human--AI
systems in which an AI does more than provide information and also participates
in determining when human judgment, external evidence, or additional
computation should intervene.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
Our contributions are threefold. First, we introduce a controlled post-answer
evaluation that freezes both answer and reported confidence before separately
varying confidence visibility, decision authority, and error cost across
32,000 matched verification decisions. Second, we show that explicitly
surfacing self-reported confidence can strongly reshape verification policy in
opposite directions across model families, cautioning against treating
reported confidence as a universally portable verification-control signal.
Third, we identify a direct divergence between confidence-policy alignment and
verification quality: for GPT-5.6 Sol, visible confidence makes downstream
actions nearly coincide with the policy implied by reported $q$ while
substantially more wrong high-cost answers remain unverified.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Self-reported uncertainty in language models.}
A growing literature studies whether language models can express useful
uncertainty about their own answers. Early work showed that models can be
trained to verbalize probabilities that are meaningfully calibrated
\citep{lin2022uncertainty}, while subsequent studies examined black-box
confidence elicitation in instruction-tuned and closed-source models
\citep{tian2023calibration,xiong2024uncertainty}. These results motivate
self-reported confidence as an accessible uncertainty signal, particularly
when token probabilities or model internals are unavailable. At the same
time, calibration is not sufficient for our setting: even a confidence score
that is informative about correctness may or may not govern a model's later
decision about whether its answer should be checked.

\paragraph{Confidence, abstention, and downstream action.}
Recent work has begun to examine this gap directly. RiskEval evaluates whether
LLMs adapt abstention behavior as the penalty for an incorrect answer changes,
finding that models can fail to convert verbal uncertainty into appropriately
risk-sensitive abstention policies \citep{wang2026faithful}. Kumaran
\citep{kumaran2026commitment} uses a two-stage answer--confidence--abstention
paradigm and finds that verbal confidence predicts subsequent commitment more
strongly than objective correctness, distinguishing verbal confidence from
token-probability-based uncertainty. More broadly, selective prediction and
abstention methods use uncertainty to trade coverage for error reduction,
including approaches that explicitly control the error rate among accepted
answers \citep{dong2026abstention}.

Our experiment is complementary to this work. Rather than asking only whether
reported confidence predicts a later decision, we freeze the answer and
reported probability and intervene on whether that same probability is
explicitly available at decision time. This permits a matched comparison of
the downstream policy with confidence hidden versus visible. It also separates
\emph{confidence-policy alignment}---whether the action agrees with the
decision rule implied by reported $q$---from \emph{verification quality}---
whether wrong answers are filtered while unnecessary verification of correct
answers is avoided.

\paragraph{Reliance and decision authority in human--AI systems.}
Research on AI-assisted decision making distinguishes mere reliance from
\emph{appropriate} reliance: accepting useful AI advice while rejecting or
scrutinizing erroneous advice \citep{schemmer2022reliance}. Interface
interventions can alter this behavior; for example, cognitive forcing
mechanisms can reduce human overreliance on incorrect AI recommendations
\citep{bucinca2021trust}. Our study does not measure human reliance. Instead,
it examines an upstream system-design question relevant to such interactions:
when an AI participates in deciding whether its own output deserves
independent scrutiny, how do its reported uncertainty and the location of
decision authority shape that recommendation?

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Dataset and models}

We use the MMLU-Pro test split \citep{wang2024mmlupro}, a
multiple-choice benchmark spanning 14 subject categories. The primary V2-B
sample contains 500 questions: the earlier 200-question V2-A sample plus 300
additional questions selected by deterministic category-stratified sampling
without reference to observed model results. The 500 questions constitute a
single primary sample; V2-A and V2-B are therefore not independent
replications. A separate 100-question category-stratified subset, also selected
without reference to outcomes, is used for prompt robustness.

We evaluate four independently developed model families: OpenAI GPT-5.6 Sol
(\texttt{gpt-5.6-sol}), Anthropic Claude Sonnet 5
(\texttt{claude-sonnet-5}), Google Gemini 3.8 Flash
(\texttt{gemini-3.8-flash}), and xAI Grok 4.20 non-reasoning
(\texttt{grok-4.20-0309-non-reasoning}). OpenAI used reasoning effort
\texttt{none}, Anthropic thinking was disabled, Gemini used thinking level
\texttt{low}, and the xAI model is explicitly non-reasoning. No tools, search,
retrieval, code execution, or external context were available. We used
provider-native structured JSON outputs and did not override temperature or
top-$p$ sampling parameters. Output was capped at 64 tokens for OpenAI,
Anthropic, and xAI. Gemini used a 512-token ceiling because its mandatory
low-thinking tokens share the output budget.

\subsection{Three-stage protocol}

Each model--question pair proceeds through three stages. In Stage~1, the model
receives the question and answer choices and returns exactly one
multiple-choice answer. This answer is then frozen. In Stage~2, the model
receives the same question, choices, and its frozen answer, is explicitly told
not to change or re-solve the answer, and reports a probability
$q\in[0,1]$ that the frozen answer is correct. This reported confidence is also
frozen.

Stage~3 does not permit the answer to change. Instead, the model selects exactly
one of two actions:
\textsc{Use\_Unverified} or \textsc{Verify\_First}. Verification has fixed
cost $C=1$ and is defined to return the correct answer. Using the frozen answer
without verification costs zero if it is correct and $L$ if it is wrong, where
\[
    L \in \{2,5,10,20\}.
\]
These costs define a stylized decision problem rather than literal real-world
harm.

For every frozen question, answer, and confidence value, Stage~3 crosses two
decision-authority framings and two confidence-visibility conditions. Under
\emph{human authority}, the prompt states that the human controls whether the
answer is independently verified before use; under \emph{AI authority}, it
states that the AI system controls verification before delivery to the human.
The prompts use matched owner-specific wording and corresponding grammar. In
the \emph{visible-confidence} condition, Stage~3 additionally states the
model's previously reported probability $q$; in the \emph{hidden-confidence}
condition, this sentence is omitted. Confidence is never re-elicited in
Stage~3. Crossing two authority conditions, two visibility conditions, and four
values of $L$ yields 16 Stage~3 decisions per model--question pair, or 32,000
primary decisions in total.

\subsection{Metrics}

We report Stage~1 accuracy and the Brier score of Stage~2 confidence separately
from Stage~3 verification behavior. For each model, authority condition,
confidence condition, and error cost, we measure the verification rate,
unverified use among actually wrong answers, and unnecessary verification among
correct answers. The latter two distinguish error filtering from verification
burden: a policy that verifies nearly everything can catch almost every error
while providing little selectivity.

We also compare Stage~3 behavior with the action implied directly by reported
confidence. Under the supplied cost model, the confidence-implied policy
verifies when
\[
    (1-q)L > C.
\]
We call agreement between the model's Stage~3 action and this action
\emph{confidence-policy alignment}, and report the corresponding disagreement
rate. This is a behavioral comparison with the model's \emph{reported}
probability; it does not assume that $q$ represents a latent internal belief.
We additionally measure stake-monotonicity violations, defined as
\textsc{Verify\_First}$\rightarrow$\textsc{Use\_Unverified} reversals as $L$
increases within an otherwise matched trajectory, as well as realized cost
under the synthetic cost model.

The confidence-visibility effect is the paired difference
\[
P(\textsc{Verify\_First}\mid\text{visible})
-
P(\textsc{Verify\_First}\mid\text{hidden}),
\]
and the owner effect is
\[
P(\textsc{Verify\_First}\mid\text{AI authority})
-
P(\textsc{Verify\_First}\mid\text{human authority}).
\]
We report models individually rather than treating the 32,000 decisions as
independent observations.

\subsection{Statistical inference and prompt robustness}

The independent sampling unit is the question. We use 5,000 question-level
paired bootstrap resamples and report nominal 95\% percentile confidence
intervals, keeping all matched factor cells for a resampled question together.
For metrics restricted to wrong answers, questions are resampled first and the
wrong-answer subset is applied within each bootstrap replicate. We do not apply
a multiplicity correction across the many descriptive cellwise comparisons;
accordingly, we emphasize effect sizes, intervals, and repeated directional
patterns rather than isolated significant cells.

Prompt robustness uses the fixed 100-question subset with the same frozen
answers and reported confidences under one semantically equivalent Stage~3
paraphrase. The preregistered robustness analysis covered the
hidden-confidence condition (3,200 decisions). After completion of the primary
experiment, we ran the corresponding visible-confidence extension (3,200
additional decisions). We therefore distinguish the hidden-condition
robustness analysis as preregistered and the visible-condition extension as
post-primary.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Baseline answer and confidence performance}

The four model families differ substantially in both answer accuracy and
reported-confidence calibration (Table~\ref{tab:baseline}). Gemini has the
highest Stage~1 accuracy at 88.0\%, followed by GPT-5.6 Sol at 82.6\%, Claude
Sonnet~5 at 74.2\%, and Grok~4.20 at 64.4\%. The corresponding numbers of wrong
answers are 60, 87, 129, and 178 out of 500, respectively. These differences
matter for interpreting verification behavior: for example, the same fraction
of wrong answers left unverified corresponds to different absolute error
burdens across models. We therefore report verification outcomes conditional
on actual correctness rather than comparing raw verification rates alone.

\subsection{Explicit confidence strongly changes verification policy}

Making the model's own frozen confidence explicit produces large changes in
verification behavior, but the direction differs sharply across model families
(Figure~\ref{fig:visibility-rates}). Claude becomes consistently more
verification-prone: across both authority conditions and all four error costs,
visible confidence increases the verification rate by 12.8--22.4 percentage
points. All eight corresponding 95\% bootstrap intervals exclude zero.

GPT-5.6 Sol moves in the opposite direction. Showing its frozen confidence
reduces verification in all eight matched authority--cost conditions, by
16.2--20.8 percentage points; again, all eight intervals exclude zero.
Grok~4.20 shows the same qualitative direction, with decreases of
8.6--16.4 points across all eight conditions.

Gemini exhibits a different cost-dependent pattern. At $L=2$, visible
confidence slightly reduces verification by 2.0--2.2 points. At $L=5$, the
effects are small and mixed across authority conditions. At the larger error
costs, however, visibility substantially increases verification: by
12.4--13.0 points at $L=10$ and 16.4--16.6 points at $L=20$, with all four
high-cost intervals excluding zero.

  \centering

  \caption{Effect of explicitly surfacing frozen self-reported confidence on
  verification rate. Points show the paired difference
  $P(\textsc{Verify\_First}\mid\mathrm{visible})-
  P(\textsc{Verify\_First}\mid\mathrm{hidden})$; error bars are nominal 95\%

  \label{fig:visibility-rates}

The consistency of these within-model directions is more important than any
single cellwise comparison. The same intervention---revealing a probability
the model itself had already reported for the same frozen answer---increases
verification by more than 20 points in some Claude conditions while decreasing
it by roughly 20 points in GPT-5.6 Sol. Self-reported confidence therefore does
not act as a model-independent verification-control signal with a common
behavioral effect. Its downstream influence depends strongly on the model
family using it.
